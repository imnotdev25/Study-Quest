🚀 Starting point

A guard model is a separate classifier that reads a prompt (input moderation) or a prompt plus response (output moderation) and returns a verdict. It lets a deployment enforce policy without retraining the main model, and it can be updated faster. Most guards today are small LLMs that generate their verdict.

Qwen3Guard (Qwen, October 2025; model card read this month): 0.6B, 4B and 8B. The Gen variants output Safety: Safe | Unsafe | Controversial, a list of Categories (Violent, Non-violent Illegal Acts, Sexual Content, PII, Suicide & Self-Harm, Unethical Acts, Politically Sensitive Topics, Copyright Violation, and Jailbreak for inputs), and for responses Refusal: Yes | No. The Stream variants add a per-token head to moderate output while it’s generated. Controversial lets you choose a strict or loose policy at deployment.

✏️ Derivation: layers help, but less than multiplication suggests

Step 1. Let layer \(i\) miss a given harmful exchange with probability \(m_i\): an input guard, the model’s own refusal, an output guard.

Step 2. If misses were independent, all three would miss with probability \(\prod_im_i\): three 10% layers give 0.1%.

Step 3. They aren’t independent: the same clever phrasing that fools one classifier tends to fool others. For two layers with miss correlation \(\rho\): \(P(\text{both miss})=m_1m_2+\rho\sqrt{m_1(1-m_1)m_2(1-m_2)}\).

Step 4. Example \(m_1=m_2=0.1\): independent gives 0.010; \(\rho=0.5\) gives \(0.01+0.5\cdot0.09=0.055\), more than 5\(\times\) worse.

Step 5. So diversify layers (different models, different training data, rules plus models), and measure the combined miss rate on attacks, not each layer alone.

🚀 Starting point

Starting point. An estimator \(\hat\theta\) is any function of the data meant to approximate an unknown \(\theta\). Because the data is random, \(\hat\theta\) is a random variable with its own distribution, the sampling distribution. Good estimators are centred on \(\theta\) (small bias) and tightly spread (small variance). Real-life version: an eval score on 500 questions is one draw from a sampling distribution. Rerun with 500 different questions and you’d get a different number.

  • Law of large numbers: \(\bar X_n\to\mu\) as \(n\to\infty\).

  • Central limit theorem: \(\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)/\sigma\to N(0,1)\) for any distribution with finite variance. The standard error of a mean is \(\sigma/\sqrt n\). For skewed data the approach is slow: the skewness of the mean is \(\gamma/\sqrt n\) (2.0, 0.88, 0.37, 0.14 in the output).

✏️ Derivation: MSE = bias\(^2\) + variance

Step 1. \(\mathrm{MSE}=\mathbb E[(\hat\theta-\theta)^2]\). Add and subtract \(\mathbb E\hat\theta\): \(\mathbb E[(\hat\theta-\mathbb E\hat\theta+\mathbb E\hat\theta-\theta)^2]\).

Step 2. The cross term is \(2(\mathbb E\hat\theta-\theta)\,\mathbb E[\hat\theta-\mathbb E\hat\theta]=0\).

Step 3. \(\boxed{\mathrm{MSE}=(\mathbb E\hat\theta-\theta)^2+\operatorname{Var}\hat\theta}\). Accepting a little bias can buy a big drop in variance.

Step 4. Example: \(\mathbb E\sum(X_i-\bar X)^2=(n-1)\sigma^2\) (one degree of freedom is used up by \(\bar X\)), so dividing by \(n-1\) is unbiased. Dividing by \(n\) (the MLE) or \(n+1\) is biased low but has lower MSE for normal data (3.57, 3.05, 2.92 in the output). Unbiased is not the same as best.

Efficiency. Two unbiased estimators of a normal’s centre: the mean and the median. The median’s variance is \(\frac\pi2\approx1.57\) times larger, so it needs 57% more data for the same precision; in exchange it is robust to outliers.

"""Inference: sampling behaviour (LLN, CLT), bias, variance and MSE of estimators."""
import math

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1. Law of large numbers: running mean of die rolls.
rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, 100_000)
for n in (10, 1000, 100_000):
    print(f"[lln] n = {n:7,d}   mean {rolls[:n].mean():.4f}   (E = 3.5)")

# 2. CLT: standardised means of a skewed Exponential(1). Skewness of the mean is 2/sqrt(n).
Phi = lambda z: 0.5 * math.erfc(-z / math.sqrt(2))
for n in (1, 5, 30, 200):
    m = rng.exponential(1, (100_000, n)).mean(1)
    z = (m - 1) * math.sqrt(n)
    print(f"[clt] n = {n:3d}   sd of mean {m.std():.4f} (1/sqrt n = {1 / math.sqrt(n):.4f})  "
          f"P(Z <= -1) {np.mean(z <= -1):.4f} (normal {Phi(-1):.4f})  skew {np.mean(((z - z.mean()) / z.std())**3):.3f}")

# 3. Variance estimators: divide by n-1 (unbiased), n (MLE), n+1 (lowest MSE for normals).
n, sigma2 = 10, 4.0
x = rng.normal(0, 2, (400_000, n))
ss = ((x - x.mean(1, keepdims=True)) ** 2).sum(1)
print("[var-est] divisor   bias      variance   MSE")
for div in (n - 1, n, n + 1):
    e = ss / div
    print(f"[var-est] n{div - n:+d}       {e.mean() - sigma2:+.4f}   {e.var():.4f}     {np.mean((e - sigma2) ** 2):.4f}")

# 4. Consistency: the sample median of a normal has variance pi/(2n) sigma^2, i.e. 57% more than the mean.
x = rng.normal(0, 1, (50_000, 101))
print(f"[eff] n=101: Var(mean) {x.mean(1).var():.5f}  Var(median) {np.median(x, 1).var():.5f}  "
      f"ratio {np.median(x, 1).var() / x.mean(1).var():.3f} (pi/2 = {math.pi / 2:.3f})")

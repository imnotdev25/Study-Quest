🚀 Starting point

The question every loss answers: which parameters make the data we observed most probable?

A model defines a probability \(p_\theta(y\mid x)\) of the target given the input. For \(N\) independent examples, the likelihood is \(\prod_ip_\theta(y_i\mid x_i)\). Products of many small numbers underflow, and products are awkward to differentiate, so take logs and flip the sign: \[\boxed{\ L(\theta)=-\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\log p_\theta(y_i\mid x_i)\ }\qquad\text{(negative log-likelihood, NLL)}\] Maximizing likelihood and minimizing NLL give the same \(\theta\), because \(\log\) is increasing. Dividing by \(N\) (mean reduction) makes the loss independent of batch size. Every loss in this book is an NLL for some choice of \(p_\theta\), or a distance between distributions.

Binary targets: Bernoulli \(\Rightarrow\) binary cross-entropy

✏️ Derivation: BCE from a coin flip

Step 1. Model: \(p_\theta(y=1\mid x)=\sigma(z)\) with logit \(z=f_\theta(x)\) and \(\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\). For \(y\in\{0,1\}\): \(p(y\mid x)=\sigma(z)^y(1-\sigma(z))^{1-y}\).

Step 2. Negative log: \(\ell=-y\log\sigma(z)-(1-y)\log(1-\sigma(z))\).

Step 3. Use \(\log\sigma(z)=-\log(1+e^{-z})\) and \(\log(1-\sigma(z))=\log\sigma(-z)=-\log(1+e^{z})\): \(\ell=y\log(1+e^{-z})+(1-y)\log(1+e^{z})\).

Step 4. Since \(\log(1+e^{-z})=\log(1+e^{z})-z\): \(\ \ell=\log(1+e^{z})-yz=\softplus(z)-yz\).

Step 5. Derivative: \(\frac{d}{dz}\softplus(z)=\sigma(z)\), so \(\ \boxed{\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z}=\sigma(z)-y}\): prediction minus target.

Step 4 is how binary_cross_entropy_with_logits computes the loss. It never forms \(\log\sigma(z)\), which would be \(\log 0=-\infty\) for a large negative \(z\).

Many classes: categorical \(\Rightarrow\) softmax cross-entropy

✏️ Derivation: cross-entropy and its famous gradient

Step 1. Model: logits \(z\in\mathbb R^C\), probabilities \(p_c=\operatorname{softmax}(z)_c=\frac{e^{z_c}}{\sum_je^{z_j}}\). They’re positive and sum to 1.

Step 2. NLL of the true class \(y\): \(\ell=-\log p_y=-z_y+\log\sum_je^{z_j}=-z_y+\LSE(z)\).

Step 3. Derivative of the log-sum-exp: \(\frac{\partial}{\partial z_c}\LSE(z)=\frac{e^{z_c}}{\sum_je^{z_j}}=p_c\).

Step 4. Derivative of \(-z_y\): \(-\mathbb 1[c=y]\).

Step 5. Together: \(\ \boxed{\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial z}=p-\onehot(y)}\). With mean reduction over a batch, divide by \(B\). This one line is the starting point of every backward pass through a language model.

Real-valued targets: Gaussian \(\Rightarrow\) mean squared error

If \(p_\theta(y\mid x)=\mathcal N(y;\mu_\theta(x),s^2)\) with fixed \(s\), then \(-\log p=\frac{(y-\mu)^2}{2s^2}+\text{const}\). The constant has no gradient, so minimizing NLL is minimizing \(\frac12(y-\mu)^2\): MSE is the NLL of a Gaussian. The critic losses in Part V are of this kind.

Distances between distributions: entropy, cross-entropy, KL

For a target distribution \(q\) and a model \(p\) over the same outcomes: \[H(q)=-\sum_cq_c\log q_c,\qquad H(q,p)=-\sum_cq_c\log p_c,\qquad D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)=\sum_cq_c\log\frac{q_c}{p_c}=H(q,p)-H(q).\] \(H(q)\) doesn’t depend on the model, so minimizing cross-entropy and minimizing KL to the target are the same thing. For a one-hot \(q\), \(H(q)=0\) and \(H(q,p)=-\log p_y\): ordinary cross-entropy. Label smoothing (Session 48) and distillation (Session 49) just use a different \(q\). KL is \(\ge0\), with equality only if \(p=q\) (Gibbs’ inequality).

🚀 Starting point

Group-relative advantage: sample \(G\) responses to the same prompt and judge each one against its siblings, \(A=\frac{R-\mu_{\text{group}}}{\sigma_{\text{group}}+\epsilon}\). No critic is needed: the group itself is the baseline. Degenerate group: all \(G\) rewards equal, so there’s nothing to compare.

Total: 20 minutes

1. Retrieval + concept refresh — 5 minutes

Quick retrieval:

  1. Why is a pairwise reward model invariant to a common score shift?

  2. Why should PPO advantages be detached before entering the policy loss?

Suppose each of \(B\) prompts produces \(G\) sampled responses:

\[R\in\mathbb{R}^{B\times G}\]

A critic-free baseline can compare each response with the other responses for the same prompt:

\[\mu_b=\frac1G\sum_{g=1}^G R_{bg}\]

\[\sigma_b= \sqrt{ \frac1G\sum_{g=1}^G(R_{bg}-\mu_b)^2 }\]

\[A_{bg}= \frac{R_{bg}-\mu_b}{\sigma_b+\epsilon} \in\mathbb{R}^{B\times G}\]

For response-token log-probabilities:

\[\ell_{\text{new}},\ell_{\text{old}},M \in\mathbb{R}^{B\times G\times T}\]

the sequence advantage is broadcast across its valid tokens:

\[A^{\text{token}}_{bgt}=A_{bg}\]

This removes the learned value model, but introduces important behavior:

  • Advantages express within-prompt rank, not absolute quality across prompts.

  • \(G=1\) or equal rewards gives zero centered advantages and no policy signal.

  • Tiny reward variance can turn insignificant reward-model noise into order-one normalized advantages.

  • A positive affine reward transform \(R'=aR+c,\ a>0\) leaves normalized advantages essentially unchanged.

  • With a token-mean loss, longer responses receive more total gradient because their advantage is repeated over more tokens.

A minimum-variance safeguard can skip unreliable groups, but its threshold must reflect reward precision and should be monitored.

✏️ Derivation: properties of group-relative advantages

Step 1. Within a group, \(\sum_g(R_g-\mu)=0\), so advantages sum to 0: some responses are pushed up exactly as much as others are pushed down.

Step 2. Degenerate group: \(R_g=\mu\) for all \(g\), so \(\sigma=0\) and \(A=\frac{0}{0+\epsilon}=0\). With \(\epsilon=0\) it’s \(0/0=\mathrm{NaN}\), which poisons the whole batch.

Step 3. Binary rewards with solve rate \(p\): the probability a group is non-degenerate is \(1-p^G-(1-p)^G\). Easy and impossible prompts mostly waste compute.

Step 4. Dividing by \(\sigma\) gives every non-degenerate group the same total “push”, regardless of how different its rewards really were (a scale invariance). Small real differences and large ones look alike.

Step 5. Each token of response \(g\) shares \(A_g\). The policy loss uses the PPO clip per token (Session 30), reduced over valid tokens.

⚙️ Algorithm: group-relative advantages
Input: rewards \(R\in\mathbb R^{B\times G}\), \(\epsilon\)
\(\mu_b\gets\frac1G\sum_gR_{bg}\); \(\sigma_b\gets\sqrt{\frac1G\sum_g(R_{bg}-\mu_b)^2}\)
\(A_{bg}\gets(R_{bg}-\mu_b)/(\sigma_b+\epsilon)\)
log the fraction of groups with \(\sigma_b=0\); drop or resample them
broadcast \(A_{bg}\) to every valid token of response \((b,g)\)

Solution — open after attempting

group_mean = rewards.mean(
    dim=1,
    keepdim=True,
)

centered_rewards = rewards - group_mean

group_variance = centered_rewards.square().mean(
    dim=1,
    keepdim=True,
)

group_std = group_variance.sqrt()

unsafe_advantages = (
    centered_rewards
    / (group_std + 1e-8)
)

active_groups = (
    group_std.squeeze(1) >= minimum_std
)

safe_advantages = torch.where(
    active_groups[:, None],
    unsafe_advantages,
    torch.zeros_like(unsafe_advantages),
)

Policy objective:

ratio = torch.exp(
    new_logp - old_logp
)

token_advantages = safe_advantages.detach().unsqueeze(-1)

policy_loss = -(
    ratio * token_advantages
)[response_mask].mean()

policy_loss.backward()

The equal-reward group has:

\[R_{bg}-\mu_b=0\]

so every advantage and policy gradient is zero.

For group 2, the standard deviation is approximately \(0.0071\). Without the safeguard, rewards differing by only \(0.01\) become advantages near:

\[[-1.414,\ 0,\ 1.414,\ 0]\]

Normalization deliberately removes scale, so tiny reward-model noise can become a full-sized update.

In group 0, reward \(4\) has the largest positive advantage. Gradient descent therefore increases its selected-token log-probabilities most strongly.

Check answers:

  1. Group-relative learning uses deviations from the group mean. Equal rewards provide no evidence that one response should become more likely than another.

  2. They remain unchanged, apart from floating-point effects: \[\frac{10(R-\mu)}{10\sigma}=A\]

  3. Inspect per-group reward standard deviation and the fraction of degenerate groups. Consider skipping or down-weighting low-variance groups, increasing \(G\), or validating reward precision.

  4. Each prompt is centered and scaled independently, so cross-prompt offsets and scales are removed.

  5. The sequence advantage is repeated at every valid token. Under a token-mean objective, total sequence contribution is therefore approximately proportional to response length.

Retrieval answers:

  • Pairwise reward loss depends only on \(r^+-r^-\), so a common score offset cancels.

  • Advantages are fixed reward estimates. Detaching them prevents unintended gradients through reward or baseline construction.

🚀 Starting point

Profile before you optimise. A profile tells you which of three things the GPU is waiting on: math (compute-bound), memory (bandwidth-bound) or the CPU (launch-bound: kernels are so small that launching them takes longer than running them).

A profiling workflow

  1. Timeline first: PyTorch profiler or Nsight Systems (nsys profile -t cuda,nvtx python serve.py). Look for gaps between kernels (CPU or launch overhead) and for which kernels dominate.

  2. One kernel deep: Nsight Compute (ncu --set full) gives achieved FLOP/s and bandwidth as fractions of peak. Compare against the roofline.

  3. Prefill vs decode: prefill should be GEMM-dominated near peak FLOP/s; decode should be near peak bandwidth. Decode at small batch often shows launch gaps, which is what CUDA graphs fix (next part).

from torch.profiler import profile, ProfilerActivity
with profile(activities=[ProfilerActivity.CPU, ProfilerActivity.CUDA], record_shapes=True) as prof:
    model.generate(**inputs, max_new_tokens=64)
print(prof.key_averages().table(sort_by="cuda_time_total", row_limit=15))
prof.export_chrome_trace("trace.json")          # open in https://ui.perfetto.dev

Tuning one GPU to an SLO

  1. Baseline with defaults at a fixed workload and rate.

  2. --gpu-memory-utilization: higher leaves more KV blocks; too high risks out-of-memory from other allocations.

  3. --max-num-seqs and --max-num-batched-tokens: the batch limit and the token budget. Raise until TPOT nears its SLO.

  4. --enable-chunked-prefill, --enable-prefix-caching (on by default in recent vLLM), FP8 weights and KV (--quantization fp8 --kv-cache-dtype fp8).

  5. Sweep the request rate and report the largest rate whose p99 TTFT and TPOT stay inside the SLO: that is the GPU’s SLO-safe capacity.

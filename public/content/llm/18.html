🚀 Starting point

Starting point. A function \(f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R\) maps inputs to a number (a loss). Its derivative along coordinate \(i\) is the limit \(\partial_if(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+he_i)-f(x)}{h}\). Stack them and you get the gradient \(\nabla f\), the direction of steepest increase. For a vector-valued \(g:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m\), the derivatives form an \(m\times n\) Jacobian. Everything below is one of these objects, checked numerically.

Derivatives you can trust: the central difference

The one-sided difference \(\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\) has error \(O(h)\). The central difference is much better:

✏️ Derivation: Why \(\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}\) has error \(O(h^2)\)

Step 1. Taylor-expand both sides: \(f(x\pm h)=f(x)\pm hf'(x)+\tfrac{h^2}{2}f''(x)\pm\tfrac{h^3}{6}f'''(x)+O(h^4)\).

Step 2. Subtract: the even terms cancel, \(f(x+h)-f(x-h)=2hf'(x)+\tfrac{h^3}{3}f'''(x)+O(h^5)\).

Step 3. Divide by \(2h\): \(\boxed{\frac{f(x+h)-f(x-h)}{2h}=f'(x)+\tfrac{h^2}{6}f'''(x)+\dots}\) With \(h=10^{-5}\) the error is around \(10^{-10}\), until floating-point rounding (about \(\epsilon_{\text{mach}}/h\)) takes over.

Chain rule and Jacobian

For \(y=\tanh(Wx)\) with \(W\in\mathbb R^{3\times4}\), write \(u=Wx\). Then \(\frac{\partial y_i}{\partial x_j}=\frac{\partial y_i}{\partial u_i}\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j}=(1-y_i^2)W_{ij}\), so \(J=\operatorname{diag}(1-y^2)\,W\). The diagonal comes from the activation acting element by element. That is exactly the Hadamard product.

Hadamard product

\((a\odot b)_i=a_ib_i\). Two facts carry through all of deep learning: \(\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\sum_i a_ib_i=b\), and a diagonal Jacobian times a vector is a Hadamard product, \(\operatorname{diag}(d)\,g=d\odot g\). That is why backprop through an activation is written \(\partial L/\partial Z=\partial L/\partial A\odot\phi'(Z)\) and never builds a matrix.

Entropy, cross-entropy and KL

Entropy \(H(p)=-\sum_ip_i\log p_i\) is the average surprise of a distribution. Cross-entropy \(\mathrm{CE}(p,q)=-\sum_ip_i\log q_i\) is your average surprise when the world follows \(p\) but you predict \(q\). KL is the extra surprise you pay:

✏️ Derivation: \(\mathrm{CE}(p,q)=H(p)+\mathrm{KL}(p\Vert q)\) and \(\mathrm{KL}\ge0\)

Step 1. \(\mathrm{KL}(p\Vert q)=\sum_ip_i\log\frac{p_i}{q_i}=\sum_ip_i\log p_i-\sum_ip_i\log q_i=-H(p)+\mathrm{CE}(p,q)\).

Step 2. Since \(H(p)\) does not depend on the model \(q\), minimising cross-entropy over \(q\) is the same as minimising KL. This is why the language-model loss is cross-entropy.

Step 3. Non-negativity: \(\log\) is concave, so by Jensen \(-\mathrm{KL}=\sum_ip_i\log\frac{q_i}{p_i}\le\log\sum_ip_i\frac{q_i}{p_i}=\log1=0\), with equality only when \(p=q\).

Estimating KL from samples (the RLHF estimator)

In RLHF you can’t sum over every possible response. You sample \(x\sim p\) and set \(r=q(x)/p(x)\).

✏️ Derivation: Two unbiased estimators: \(k_1=-\log r\) and \(k_3=(r-1)-\log r\)

Step 1. \(\mathbb E_p[-\log r]=\sum_xp(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}=\mathrm{KL}(p\Vert q)\). Unbiased, but individual samples are negative whenever \(q(x)>p(x)\).

Step 2. \(\mathbb E_p[r-1]=\sum_xq(x)-1=0\), so adding \(r-1\) keeps the mean and acts as a control variate.

Step 3. \(r-1-\log r\ge0\) for every \(r>0\) (the tangent line of \(\log\) at 1 lies above it). Each sample is non-negative, and the variance drops a lot when \(p\approx q\).

SVD and the best low-rank approximation

Every matrix factors as \(M=U\Sigma V^\top\) with orthonormal columns in \(U,V\) and \(\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\dots\ge0\). Keeping the top \(k\) terms gives \(M_k=\sum_{i\le k}\sigma_iu_iv_i^\top\), and the Eckart–Young theorem says no rank-\(k\) matrix is closer: \(\lVert M-M_k\rVert_F=\sqrt{\sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2}\). LoRA (Toolkit II) bets that the weight update of a fine-tune has a fast-decaying spectrum like this.

EMA and bias correction

\(m_t=\beta m_{t-1}+(1-\beta)x_t\) with \(m_0=0\). Unrolling, \(m_t=(1-\beta)\sum_{s=1}^t\beta^{t-s}x_s\). If every \(x_s=c\), the sum is a geometric series: \(m_t=c(1-\beta^t)\). So early averages are biased toward zero, and dividing by \(1-\beta^t\) removes it exactly. Adam does this for both of its moments.

L1 vs L2: why L1 makes zeros

✏️ Derivation: Minimise \(\tfrac12(w-c)^2+\text{penalty}\)

Step 1. L2, penalty \(\tfrac\lambda2w^2\): set the derivative \(w-c+\lambda w\) to 0, giving \(w=\frac{c}{1+\lambda}\). Every weight shrinks by the same factor and none reaches zero.

Step 2. L1, penalty \(\lambda|w|\): for \(w>0\) the derivative is \(w-c+\lambda\), so \(w=c-\lambda\), valid only if \(c>\lambda\). Symmetrically for \(w<0\). If \(|c|\le\lambda\), zero is the minimum because the subgradient \([-\lambda,\lambda]\) of \(|w|\) at 0 can absorb \(-c\).

Step 3. Result: the soft threshold \(\boxed{w=\operatorname{sign}(c)\max(|c|-\lambda,0)}\).

With \(\lambda=0.6\): L2 tilts the line, L1 cuts out a flat zero band of width \(2\lambda\).

"""Toolkit ch. 1-2: the math fundamentals, each checked numerically."""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1. Derivatives and gradients: central differences agree with the analytic gradient.
f = lambda x: np.sum(x**2 * np.sin(x))
grad_f = lambda x: 2 * x * np.sin(x) + x**2 * np.cos(x)
x = rng.normal(size=5)
h = 1e-5
num = np.array([(f(x + h * e) - f(x - h * e)) / (2 * h) for e in np.eye(5)])
print(f"[grad] max |numeric - analytic| = {np.abs(num - grad_f(x)).max():.1e}")

# 2. Chain rule and Jacobian: y = tanh(Wx), J = diag(1 - y^2) W.
W = rng.normal(size=(3, 4))
y = np.tanh(W @ x[:4])
J = (1 - y**2)[:, None] * W
J_num = np.stack([(np.tanh(W @ (x[:4] + h * e)) - np.tanh(W @ (x[:4] - h * e))) / (2 * h)
                  for e in np.eye(4)], axis=1)
print(f"[jacobian] shape {J.shape}, max err {np.abs(J - J_num).max():.1e}")

# 3. Hadamard product: d(sum(a*b))/da = b, element by element.
a, b = rng.normal(size=4), rng.normal(size=4)
g_num = np.array([(np.sum((a + h * e) * b) - np.sum((a - h * e) * b)) / (2 * h) for e in np.eye(4)])
print(f"[hadamard] a*b = {np.round(a * b, 3)}  numeric grad wrt a == b: {np.allclose(g_num, b)}")

# 4. Entropy, cross-entropy, KL:  CE(p, q) = H(p) + KL(p || q).
p = np.array([0.7, 0.2, 0.1])
q = np.array([0.4, 0.4, 0.2])
H = -(p * np.log(p)).sum()
CE = -(p * np.log(q)).sum()
KL = (p * np.log(p / q)).sum()
print(f"[info] H(p)={H:.4f}  CE(p,q)={CE:.4f}  KL(p||q)={KL:.4f}  H+KL={H + KL:.4f}")
print(f"[info] KL(q||p)={(q * np.log(q / p)).sum():.4f}  (KL is not symmetric)")

# 5. Monte Carlo KL estimators used in RLHF (samples from p, r = q/p):
#    k1 = -log r (unbiased, can go negative), k3 = (r - 1) - log r (unbiased, always >= 0).
s = rng.choice(3, size=200_000, p=p)
r = q[s] / p[s]
k1, k3 = -np.log(r), (r - 1) - np.log(r)
print(f"[kl-est] k1 mean {k1.mean():.4f} std {k1.std():.3f} | k3 mean {k3.mean():.4f} std {k3.std():.3f}")

# 6. SVD and Eckart-Young: the best rank-k approximation keeps the top k singular values,
#    and its Frobenius error is sqrt(sum of the dropped sigma^2).
M = rng.normal(size=(50, 4)) @ rng.normal(size=(4, 40)) + 0.05 * rng.normal(size=(50, 40))
U, S, Vt = np.linalg.svd(M, full_matrices=False)
for k in (1, 4, 10):
    Mk = (U[:, :k] * S[:k]) @ Vt[:k]
    print(f"[svd] rank {k:2d}: error {np.linalg.norm(M - Mk):7.3f}  predicted {np.sqrt((S[k:]**2).sum()):7.3f}")
print(f"[svd] top 6 singular values {np.round(S[:6], 2)}  (rank-4 signal + noise)")

# 7. EMA: m_t = beta m_{t-1} + (1 - beta) x_t. Bias correction divides by 1 - beta^t.
beta, m = 0.9, 0.0
for t, xt in enumerate([5.0] * 5, start=1):
    m = beta * m + (1 - beta) * xt
    print(f"[ema] t={t}  raw {m:.3f}  corrected {m / (1 - beta**t):.3f}")

# 8. L1 vs L2: minimise 0.5 (w - c)^2 + penalty.
#    L2 (lam/2 w^2): w = c / (1 + lam)  shrinks everything.  L1 (lam |w|): soft threshold, zeros small weights.
c, lam = np.array([3.0, 0.5, -0.2, 1.2]), 0.6
print(f"[norms] c        {c}")
print(f"[norms] ridge    {np.round(c / (1 + lam), 3)}")
print(f"[norms] lasso    {np.round(np.sign(c) * np.maximum(np.abs(c) - lam, 0), 3)}")

🔑 Key ideas

  • Check every derivative with a central difference. It’s the unit test of calculus.

  • Cross-entropy = entropy + KL. Minimising the LM loss is minimising KL to the data.

  • \(k_3\) has the same mean as \(k_1\) here (0.184) at a seventh of the standard deviation.

  • The error columns of the SVD table match Eckart–Young to three decimals.

🧩 Analogy: a rubber band to your old self

Picture a rubber band tying you to who you were. You can stretch toward new goals, but the further you go, the harder it pulls back. KL regularization ties the policy to the reference model this way: it can chase reward, but each step away from trusted behaviour costs something. It doesn’t say what’s good. It limits how far you’ll go to get it.

2. Keep the policy near a trusted starting point

Let \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) be the frozen SFT model and \(\pi_\theta\) the trainable policy.

Reward-only optimization asks:

\[\max_\theta \mathbb E_{y\sim\pi_\theta} [r_\phi(x,y)]\]

KL-regularized RLHF instead asks:

\[\max_\theta \mathbb E_{y\sim\pi_\theta} [r_\phi(x,y)] - \beta D_{\mathrm{KL}} \left( \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x) \| \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x) \right)\]

The KL term measures how much the trainable policy has moved away from the reference:

\[D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}}) = \mathbb E_{y\sim\pi_\theta} \left[ \log\pi_\theta(y\mid x) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) \right]\]

Think of the reference policy as an elastic tether. Reward pulls toward higher-scoring responses; KL pulls toward behaviour already learned during SFT.

The role of \(\beta\)

  • Small \(\beta\): reward dominates; larger policy movement.

  • Large \(\beta\): reference behaviour dominates.

  • \(\beta=0\): reward-only optimization.

  • Reward rescaling without adjusting \(\beta\) changes the trade-off.

For a fixed reward over a finite response space, the optimal policy is:

\[\pi^*(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x) \exp\left(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\right)\]

A high reward increases probability, but the reference probability still matters.

Token-level implementation

For a generated response:

\[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)} {\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} = \sum_t M_t \left[ \log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid s_t) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t\mid s_t) \right]\]

A sampled token-level KL penalty is often incorporated into reward:

\[r_t^{\text{shaped}} = r_t - \beta \left( \log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid s_t) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t\mid s_t) \right)\]

Individual sampled log-ratios can be negative. Only their expectation under \(\pi_\theta\) equals the nonnegative forward KL.

Prompt and padding positions must be masked. Summing KL over tokens also means longer responses can accumulate larger penalties, so reduction conventions matter.

Two different anchors

Do not confuse:

  • Reference KL: current policy versus fixed SFT model; preserves behaviour.

  • PPO old-policy KL: current policy versus rollout policy; controls update stability.

They may both be monitored in one training run.

DPO connection

DPO places policy-versus-reference log-ratios directly inside a pairwise preference loss. It can be derived from a KL-regularized preference objective, but it trains on fixed preference pairs rather than reward-scored online rollouts.

🔍 Deeper: the closed-form optimal policy

For each prompt, \(\max_\pi\ \mathbb E_{y\sim\pi}[r(y)]-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\) has the solution \[\pi^*(y)=\frac{1}{Z}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\,e^{r(y)/\beta},\qquad Z=\sum_y\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)e^{r(y)/\beta}.\] Proof in one line: \(\mathbb E_\pi[r]-\beta\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}})=-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi^*)+\beta\log Z\), which is maximized exactly when \(\pi=\pi^*\).

Example: three responses with \(\pi_{\text{ref}}=(0.6,0.3,0.1)\) and rewards \(r=(0,1,2)\):

Small \(\beta\): the rare high-reward response takes over. Large \(\beta\): back to the reference. Rearranging gives \(r=\beta\log\frac{\pi^*}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}+\beta\log Z\), which is the seed of DPO.

🔑 Key ideas

  • The optimal KL-regularized policy is the reference, tilted by \(e^{r/\beta}\).

  • \(\beta\) controls how much trusted behaviour you’ll trade for reward.

🚶 Walkthrough: \(\beta=1\)

\(\pi_{\text{ref}}=(0.6,0.3,0.1)\), \(r=(0,1,2)\). Weights \(0.6e^0,\ 0.3e^1,\ 0.1e^2=(0.600,0.815,0.739)\), \(Z=2.154\), so \(\pi^*=(0.279,0.379,0.343)\). The rare response \(C\) grew from 10% to 34%. Reward pulled it up, and KL kept it from taking over.

Common mistakes

  • Exponentiating before adding logs (overflow for large \(r/\beta\)).

  • Treating \(\beta\) as “reward strength” (it’s the inverse: smaller \(\beta\) means more reward-chasing).

  • Comparing \(\beta\) across reward models with different scales.

⚙️ Algorithm: Exact KL-regularized policy over a candidate set
Input: candidates \(y_1,\dots,y_n\), reference probabilities \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i)\), rewards \(r(y_i)\), \(\beta>0\)
for \(i=1,\dots,n\) do
\(\ell_i\gets\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_i)+r(y_i)/\beta\) ▷ work in log space
end
\(\pi^*\gets\operatorname{softmax}(\ell_1,\dots,\ell_n)\) ▷ \(=\pi_{\text{ref}}e^{r/\beta}/Z\)
return \(\pi^*\)

Hints

  • Use F.log_softmax.

  • Expected reward is the probability-weighted reward sum.

  • Forward KL uses policy_probs * (policy_log_probs - reference_log_probs).

  • Maximize reward minus beta * KL.

  • Closed-form optimal logits are reference_log_probs + reward / beta.

  • Softmax ignores a shared additive constant.

Debugging experiments

Use the wrong KL sign:

objective = (
    expected_reward
    + beta * kl_to_reference
)

The optimizer is now rewarded for moving away from the reference. KL can grow even when that movement provides little extra reward.

Then remove .detach() from a reference model that shares an active computation graph. Gradients may update both the policy and its anchor, allowing the anchor to follow the policy instead of constraining it.

Solutions

Current probabilities:

policy_log_probs = F.log_softmax(
    policy_logits,
    dim=-1,
)

policy_probs = policy_log_probs.exp()

Expected reward:

expected_reward = (
    policy_probs * reward_scores
).sum()

Forward KL:

kl_to_reference = (
    policy_probs
    * (
        policy_log_probs
        - reference_log_probs
    )
).sum()

Regularized objective:

objective = (
    expected_reward
    - beta * kl_to_reference
)

loss = -objective

Closed-form optimum:

optimal_logits = (
    reference_log_probs
    + reward_scores / beta
)

theoretical_probs = torch.softmax(
    optimal_logits,
    dim=-1,
)

Initially, the trainable and reference policies are identical:

\[D_{\mathrm{KL}} (\pi_{\text{ref}}\|\pi_{\text{ref}}) =0\]

The reference logits are:

\[[1,0,-1]\]

With \(\beta=0.5\):

\[\frac{r}{\beta}=[0,2,4]\]

Therefore, the optimal logits are equivalent to:

\[[1,0,-1]+[0,2,4]=[1,2,3]\]

and:

\[\pi^*=\operatorname{softmax}([1,2,3])\]

Approximately:

response 0: 0.090
response 1: 0.245
response 2: 0.665

Response 2 becomes most likely, but does not reach probability one. The KL penalty preserves meaningful probability on responses favoured by the reference.

A sampled log-ratio can be negative:

\[\log\pi_\theta(y)-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)<0\]

for responses whose probability decreased. Nevertheless:

\[\mathbb E_{y\sim\pi_\theta} \left[ \log\pi_\theta(y)-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y) \right] = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}}) \geq0\]

Check answers

  1. It acts as a prior or behavioural anchor: high-reference-probability responses are cheaper to retain.

  2. KL measures distributional change, not factuality, helpfulness or safety.

  3. Reward overwhelms the unchanged penalty, making the effective constraint much weaker.

  4. Each generated token adds another log-ratio term, so longer responses have more opportunities to accumulate KL.

Retrieval answers

  1. Reward-only optimization keeps increasing the probability of whatever the frozen scorer ranks highest, driving entropy toward zero.

  2. DPO trains directly on fixed preference pairs; reward-model RLHF scores newly generated rollouts and performs policy optimization.

Takeaway: KL regularization does not tell the policy what is good. It limits how much trusted behaviour the policy may trade away while pursuing learned reward.

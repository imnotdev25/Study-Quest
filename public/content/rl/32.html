🧩 Analogy: Goodhart’s law and the cobra effect

“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” A classic (likely apocryphal) story: a city paid a bounty per dead cobra, so people started breeding cobras. Social apps that optimize watch-time can drift into the same trap, from “content you enjoy” to “content you can’t stop watching.” An RL policy is the most relentless bounty-hunter of all.

Stylized overoptimization curve, shaped like Gao et al. (2022). Proxy keeps rising, true quality peaks and then falls.

2. The policy optimizes exactly what it can see

Suppose the desired behaviour has unknown true utility:

\[U(x,y)\]

Because \(U\) is difficult to measure, training uses a proxy reward:

\[R(x,y)\approx U(x,y)\]

The policy then maximizes:

\[J(\theta) = \mathbb E_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[R(x,y)]\]

This works only while higher proxy reward reliably means higher true utility.

During optimization, the policy actively searches the response space. It may discover unusual responses where:

\[R(x,y)\gg U(x,y)\]

This is reward hacking or specification gaming: the policy obtains high measured reward without delivering the intended outcome.

A familiar analogy is teaching to a test. The score initially measures understanding, but intense optimization may discover memorization, guessing patterns or leaked answers.

Three different failures

Optimization failure

\[R\text{ does not increase}\]

The RL algorithm, gradients or implementation may be broken.

Generalization failure

Reward rises on training prompts but not on held-out prompts.

Specification failure

\[R\uparrow,\qquad U\downarrow\]

Training successfully optimizes the wrong behaviour.

Examples in LLM systems include:

  • verbosity rewarded as a proxy for helpfulness;

  • confident wording mistaken for correctness;

  • reward models preferring familiar answer templates;

  • code exploiting incomplete tests;

  • tool agents claiming success without checking tool state;

  • models learning evaluator-specific phrases.

Goodhart’s law in optimization form

A correlation observed in ordinary data may fail at extremes selected by optimization:

\[\operatorname{corr}(R,U)>0 \quad\not\Rightarrow\quad \arg\max_y R(y)=\arg\max_y U(y)\]

The policy eventually visits outputs unlike those used to design or validate the reward.

Useful defenses include:

  • hidden and adversarial evaluations;

  • independent metrics not used for training;

  • human audits of highest-reward samples;

  • reward-model ensembles or uncertainty estimates;

  • KL and update limits;

  • refreshing reward data using current-policy outputs;

  • logging proxy reward and real task metrics separately.

DPO can also overfit annotation biases or superficial chosen-response patterns. It does not require an online reward model, but its preference objective remains a proxy for human intent.

🔍 Deeper: why optimization finds the errors

Write the proxy as \(\hat r(y)=r(y)+e(y)\), true reward plus error. Pick the best of \(N\) samples by \(\hat r\): you select for high \(r\) and high \(e\). If \(e\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\) independently, then \(\mathbb E[e\text{ of the winner}]\approx\sigma\sqrt{2\ln N}\), which grows with optimization pressure and never stops.

RL is a much stronger optimizer than best-of-\(N\), so it finds rare outputs where \(e\) is huge: odd formatting, length padding, sycophancy, checker exploits. Watch for: reward rising fast, entropy collapsing, KL growing, responses getting longer or weirder. Read the top-reward samples by hand.

🔑 Key ideas

  • Optimizing a proxy amplifies exactly the proxy’s errors.

  • Rising reward is a reason to inspect outputs, not to celebrate.

🚶 Walkthrough: how much noise does best-of-16 select?

If the proxy error \(e\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\), the selected sample’s expected error is \(\mathbb E[\max_{16}Z]\approx1.77\) (by simulation; the \(\sqrt{2\ln N}\) formula gives 2.36, an overestimate at small \(N\)). So about 1.77 standard deviations of “score” come from judge error alone.

Common mistakes

  • Early-stopping on training reward.

  • Using the same reward model for training and evaluation.

  • Reading only random samples, never the top-reward ones.

4. Checks for understanding

  1. Why does more optimization pressure often reveal more reward hacking?

  2. Why is evaluating random or SFT-model samples insufficient?

  3. Can KL regularization eliminate reward hacking?

  4. What evidence distinguishes an optimization bug from a specification bug?

Solutions

Sampling and rewards:

sampled_responses = policy.sample(
    (batch_size,)
)

rewards = proxy_rewards[
    sampled_responses
]

Advantages:

advantages = rewards - rewards.mean()

advantages = advantages / (
    rewards.std(unbiased=False) + 1e-8
)

Policy-gradient loss:

selected_log_probs = policy.log_prob(
    sampled_responses
)

policy_loss = -(
    selected_log_probs
    * advantages.detach()
).mean()

Independent metrics:

expected_proxy = (
    probabilities * proxy_rewards
).sum()

expected_true = (
    probabilities * true_utilities
).sum()

Initially, the policy is uniform. Its expected metrics are:

\[\mathbb E[R] = \frac{0+1+2+3+5}{5} = 2.2\]

\[\mathbb E[U] = \frac{0+1+2+1-2}{5} = 0.4\]

Because response 4 has the greatest proxy reward, training moves toward:

\[\pi(4)\rightarrow1\]

Consequently:

\[\mathbb E[R]\rightarrow5\]

but:

\[\mathbb E[U]\rightarrow-2\]

The optimizer is functioning correctly. The reward specification is failing.

Response 2 has the best true utility, but the policy has no access to that fact during training.

Check answers

  1. Stronger optimization searches farther from ordinary training examples and is more likely to find proxy–utility mismatches.

  2. Hacking may appear only after the optimized policy deliberately concentrates on extreme high-reward outputs.

  3. No. KL can limit how quickly or how far the policy moves, but it does not make an incorrect reward correct.

  4. If proxy reward rises as intended while independent task metrics fall, the optimization works but the specification is wrong.

Retrieval answers

  1. GRPO changes how advantages are estimated; it still optimizes the supplied reward and can exploit its errors.

  2. A reward model predicts quality from learned data, while a verifier executes a rule or test. Either can still be incomplete or exploitable.

Takeaway: reward is a training signal, not proof of success. A Research Engineer should treat rapidly rising reward—especially with falling entropy or growing distribution shift—as a reason to inspect high-reward outputs, not automatically celebrate.

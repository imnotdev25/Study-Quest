🧩 Analogy: likes shape what you post

Post a photo, get lots of likes, and you’ll post more photos like it. Get none, and you’ll post fewer. You didn’t calculate “the gradient of likes with respect to photo style.” You just made rewarded behaviour more likely. That’s a policy gradient: actions that were followed by reward become more probable.

A uniform softmax policy samples \(a_2\) and gets \(R=2\). One gradient step (learning rate 0.5) shifts probability toward \(a_2\).

2. Learn the policy directly

DQN first learns action values and then chooses:

\[a=\arg\max_a Q(s,a)\]

A policy-gradient method directly learns a probability distribution:

\[\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\]

For discrete actions, a network produces logits:

\[z_\theta(s)\in\mathbb R^A\]

Softmax converts them into probabilities:

\[\pi_\theta(a\mid s) = \frac{\exp(z_a)} {\sum_j\exp(z_j)}\]

For a batch:

  • logits: [B, actions]

  • sampled actions: [B]

  • selected log-probabilities: [B]

  • rewards: [B]

The sampling problem

Sampling an action is discrete:

action = distribution.sample()

We cannot backpropagate through the sampled integer. The log-probability trick gives another route:

\[\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb E_{a\sim\pi_\theta} \left[ R(a)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a) \right]\]

To use gradient descent, minimize:

\[L(\theta) = -\mathbb E \left[ R(a)\log\pi_\theta(a) \right]\]

Interpret it as reward-weighted cross-entropy:

  • ordinary cross-entropy increases the probability of a labelled answer;

  • policy-gradient loss increases the probability of a sampled action when its reward is positive;

  • negative reward decreases its probability.

Why the negative sign?

Optimizers minimize losses, but RL wants to maximize reward.

If \(R>0\):

\[L=-R\log\pi(a)\]

Minimizing this makes \(\log\pi(a)\) larger, meaning the sampled action becomes more probable.

If \(R<0\), the update reverses and reduces that action’s probability.

Line-slope connection

A derivative measures how an objective changes when one parameter moves. A gradient collects one derivative per parameter:

\[\nabla_\theta J = \left[ \frac{\partial J}{\partial\theta_1}, \ldots, \frac{\partial J}{\partial\theta_n} \right]\]

The policy-gradient estimator uses sampled data to approximate that unknown expected slope:

\[\widehat{\nabla_\theta J} = \frac1B\sum_{i=1}^{B} R_i\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_i)\]

It is unbiased under standard assumptions, but can have high variance.

LLM connection

For an LLM, the policy is the next-token distribution. The same structure appears:

\[-\text{reward}\times \log\pi_\theta(\text{sampled token}\mid\text{prefix})\]

Later we will handle multiple tokens, delayed sequence rewards and credit assignment.

🔍 Deeper: the log-derivative trick

We want \(\nabla_\theta\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi_\theta}[R(a)]\), but sampling isn’t differentiable. Use \(\nabla\pi=\pi\nabla\log\pi\): \[\nabla_\theta\sum_a\pi_\theta(a)R(a)=\sum_a\pi_\theta(a)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a)\,R(a)=\mathbb E_{a\sim\pi_\theta}\big[R(a)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a)\big].\] Now it’s an expectation we can sample, and that gives an unbiased gradient estimate.

Softmax case: with \(\pi=\operatorname{softmax}(z)\), \(\nabla_z\log\pi(a)=e_a-\pi\) (a one-hot vector minus the probabilities).

Worked example (the chart): \(z=0\), so \(\pi=(\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13)\). Sample \(a_2\) with \(R=2\): gradient \(=2\big((0,1,0)-\pi\big)=(-\tfrac23,\tfrac43,-\tfrac23)\). After a step of 0.5: \(z=(-\tfrac13,\tfrac23,-\tfrac13)\), so \(\pi\approx(0.212,0.576,0.212)\).

🔑 Key ideas

  • \(\nabla J=\mathbb E[R\,\nabla\log\pi]\): push up log-probability in proportion to reward.

  • You never differentiate through the environment or the sampling step.

🚶 Walkthrough: a negative reward pushes probability away

Uniform policy over 3 actions, sample \(a_2\), \(R=-1\), step 0.5. Gradient \(=-1\cdot(e_2-\pi)=(\tfrac13,-\tfrac23,\tfrac13)\). Logits become \((\tfrac16,-\tfrac13,\tfrac16)\), so \(\pi\approx(0.384,0.233,0.384)\). The punished action loses probability, and the others share the gain equally.

Common mistakes

  • Minimizing \(R\log\pi\) instead of \(-R\log\pi\) (the sign flip learns the opposite).

  • Using probabilities instead of log-probabilities.

  • Feeding argmax actions instead of sampled actions into the loss.

⚙️ Algorithm: Policy gradient for a softmax bandit
Input: logits \(z\in\mathbb R^k\), learning rate \(\eta\)
for each iteration do
\(\pi\gets\operatorname{softmax}(z)\); sample \(a\sim\pi\); observe reward \(R\)
\(z\gets z+\eta\,R\,(e_a-\pi)\) ▷ \(=\ z+\eta R\nabla_z\log\pi(a)\)
end

Debugging experiment

Remove the negative sign:

loss = (log_probs * rewards.detach()).mean()

The optimizer now minimizes expected reward: actions receiving positive rewards become less probable.

Also prefer:

Categorical(logits=logits)

over manually calculating torch.log(softmax(logits)). The distribution API handles log-probabilities more stably.

4. Checks for understanding

  1. Why can’t we simply backpropagate through the sampled action index?

  2. What does a positive reward do to the sampled action’s probability?

  3. If all rewards are multiplied by 1000, what happens to gradient scale?

  4. Why can one batch produce a gradient pointing in an unhelpful direction?

Solutions

actions = policy.sample((batch_size,))
log_probs = policy.log_prob(actions)
loss = -(
    log_probs * rewards.detach()
).mean()

After loss.backward():

gradient = logits.grad.detach().clone()

The final policy should strongly prefer action 1:

P(action 0) → near 0
P(action 1) → near 1

At initialization:

\[\operatorname{softmax}([0,0])=[0.5,0.5]\]

The probabilities need not improve monotonically. Rewards and actions are sampled, so individual gradient estimates are noisy.

For two-action softmax, raising one action’s relative probability lowers the other’s. Consequently, their logit gradients usually have opposite signs and approximately sum to zero.

Check answers

  1. A sampled integer is a discrete operation without the ordinary differentiable path required by backpropagation.

  2. It increases the sampled action’s log-probability under gradient descent.

  3. Gradients become roughly 1000 times larger, which may destabilize training unless rewards or learning rates are rescaled.

  4. A finite sample can contain unusually lucky bad actions or unusually unlucky good actions.

Retrieval answers

  1. No. DQN learns action values and derives a policy using operations such as argmax.

  2. A greedy value policy would otherwise keep choosing its current favourite and might never discover better actions.

Takeaway: policy gradients do not differentiate through sampled actions. They use reward-weighted log-probabilities to answer: “Should actions like the one I sampled become more or less likely?”

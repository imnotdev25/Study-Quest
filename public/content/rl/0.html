🧩 Analogy: doomscrolling is reward maxing

Your brain is an RL agent. Opening a short-video app gives an instant reward (a little hit of dopamine). Going to the gym gives an immediate negative reward (effort, sweat), but a big positive return weeks later.

Doomscrolling is what happens when an agent optimizes the reward instead of the return. RL is the study of agents that learn to care about the whole trip, not just the next step.

The loop every RL problem shares: act, observe, get rewarded, repeat.

1. The idea: a good first move can look unrewarding

Imagine choosing a route home. One road gets you moving immediately but leads into traffic. Another starts with a slow detour but gets you home sooner.

Reinforcement learning asks: Which way of choosing actions produces the best total outcome—not just the best immediate result?

Here’s a tiny environment. Rewards are invented points, not minutes:

RL term Meaning Route example
Agent The decision-maker You
Environment What responds to actions Roads and traffic
State \(s\) Information describing the current situation At the starting junction
Action \(a\) A choice available now Main road or detour
Policy \(\pi(a\mid s)\) Rule assigning probabilities to actions Choose detour with probability 0.7
Reward \(r_{t+1}\) Feedback after an action Points received on that step
Return \(G_t\) Accumulated future reward Total points across the trip

Suppose the two routes produce these reward sequences:

  • Main road: \(+2,-4\)

  • Detour: \(0,+3\)

The main road wins immediately. The detour wins over the whole trip.

For today, use an undiscounted return—just add the rewards:

\[G_t=\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}r_{k+1}\]

A policy’s objective is its expected return:

\[J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[G_0]\]

“Expected” means a probability-weighted average, like calculating an average exam score when different outcomes have different chances.

If \(p\) is the probability of choosing the detour:

\[J(p)=(1-p)G_{\text{main}}+pG_{\text{detour}}\]

This is a straight line in \(p\). Its slope tells you how expected return changes when you increase the probability of the detour. That slope is not an optimizer: a learning algorithm must still decide how to change the policy.

Our example has only one meaningful decision, followed by a fixed outcome. General RL includes repeated decisions that change future states.

LLM connection: A token can look locally plausible yet lead to a poor final answer. A sequence-level reward evaluates the completed outcome; deciding which earlier choices deserve credit is a later topic.

🔍 Deeper: why the best policy here is deterministic

With detour probability \(p\), \(J(p)=(1-p)(-2)+p(3)=-2+5p\).

  • The slope \(dJ/dp = G_{\text{detour}}-G_{\text{main}} = 5\). A linear objective is maximized at an endpoint, so with known returns the optimal policy is deterministic. Randomness only becomes useful when you still need to learn the returns (next topic).

  • Shifting probability toward the better outcome, in proportion to how much better it is, is exactly what policy gradients do later.

  • Sampling error: route returns are \(\{-2,3\}\). At \(p=0.8\) the variance is \(p(1-p)\cdot 5^2=4\), so the standard error of a mean of \(n\) episodes is \(2/\sqrt{n}\): about \(0.45\) at \(n=20\), and \(0.045\) at \(n=2000\). That’s why 20 episodes look noisy.

🔑 Key ideas

  • Reward = one step. Return \(G_t\) = everything after. Objective \(J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_\pi[G_0]\).

  • Sample means converge at rate \(1/\sqrt{n}\): 100\(\times\) more episodes buys 10\(\times\) less noise.

🚶 Walkthrough: five sampled episodes

Policy: detour with \(p=0.7\). Exact \(J=0.3(-2)+0.7(3)=1.5\). Five sampled routes: detour, main, detour, detour, detour \(\Rightarrow\) returns \(3,-2,3,3,3\), mean \(=2.0\). The sample mean misses by \(0.5\). With standard error \(\approx2.29/\sqrt5\approx1.0\), that miss is normal, not a bug.

Common mistakes

  • Averaging the first reward instead of the full return.

  • Summing over the wrong tensor dimension (routes instead of steps).

  • Calling a policy “better” from one or two episodes.

⚙️ Algorithm: Monte Carlo estimate of a policy’s expected return
Input: policy \(\pi\), number of episodes \(N\)
for \(i=1,\dots,N\) do
run one episode with \(\pi\) and record its rewards \(r_1,\dots,r_T\)
\(G^{(i)}\gets\sum_{t=1}^{T}r_t\)
end
return \(\hat J(\pi)=\frac1N\sum_iG^{(i)}\) ▷ standard error \(\approx\operatorname{sd}(G)/\sqrt N\)

3. Checks for understanding

  1. Why is choosing the largest first reward wrong in the original environment?

  2. Does a policy choosing detour with probability 0.8 guarantee exactly 16 detours in 20 episodes?

  3. A colleague uses rewards.mean(dim=0) to calculate route returns. What does that actually average?

  4. If sampled mean return differs from expected return, does that alone indicate a bug?

  5. In this example, what is the slope \(dJ/dp\), and what does its sign tell you?

Solutions — read after attempting

route_returns = rewards.sum(dim=1)
expected_returns = policies @ route_returns

# Inside the loop:
estimated_return = route_returns[actions].mean()

Exact results:

Route returns:          tensor([-2.,  3.])
Expected policy returns: tensor([-1.,  2.])

With only 20 episodes, sampling estimates will generally fluctuate more. A fixed seed makes a run reproducible; it does not eliminate sampling error.

Changing the main road’s final reward to 4.0 gives route returns [6, 3] and policy expectations [5.4, 3.6]. The main-road-heavy policy becomes better.

Check answers

  1. It ignores the later penalty: total outcomes are \(-2\) versus \(3\).

  2. No. Sixteen is the expected count, not a guaranteed count.

  3. Across routes at each step—not across steps for each route. The result even has the expected shape here, making this a subtle bug.

  4. No. Random sampling produces estimation error.

  5. \(J(p)=-2+5p\), so the slope is \(5\). Increasing detour probability increases expected return.

Takeaway: Reward scores one step. Return scores what follows. A policy controls which outcomes you tend to experience.

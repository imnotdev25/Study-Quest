🧩 Analogy: a comedian testing new jokes

A comedian tries new jokes (sample), watches the audience’s laughter (score), and keeps more of what gets laughs (update). But if they only chase laughs from one rowdy person, the act drifts somewhere weird. So they stay close to their own style. Those are RLHF’s three parts: sample, score with a reward model, update with a KL leash.

2. Where reinforcement learning enters RLHF

A typical reward-model-based RLHF pipeline has three learned components:

  • SFT/reference model: provides a capable starting policy.

  • Reward model: predicts human preference for a response.

  • RL policy: generates new responses and is optimized using their predicted rewards.

The recurring policy-training loop is:

  1. sample prompts \(x\);

  2. generate responses \(y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\);

  3. calculate \(R=r_\phi(x,y)\);

  4. estimate advantages;

  5. update \(\pi_\theta\);

  6. discard or carefully limit reuse of stale rollouts;

  7. repeat with newly generated responses.

The central objective is:

\[J(\theta) = \mathbb E_{ x\sim\mathcal D,\, y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x) } [r_\phi(x,y)]\]

A simple policy loss is:

\[L_{\text{policy}} = -\frac1B \sum_b \hat A_b \log\pi_\theta(y_b\mid x_b)\]

In a real LLM:

\[\log\pi_\theta(y_b\mid x_b) = \sum_t M_{b,t} \log\pi_\theta(y_{b,t}\mid x_b,y_{b,<t})\]

Why this is online RL

The reward model may be frozen, but the policy repeatedly generates fresh responses. As the policy changes, the training distribution changes:

\[y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{new}}} \neq y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\]

Here, “online” means policy-generated interaction during training. It does not mean a human must label every new response immediately.

Do not backpropagate through reward

The reward model’s score is supervision for a sampled discrete response:

reward = reward_model(prompt, response)

The policy gradient does not differentiate through the sampled token IDs or through the reward model. It uses:

\[R\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\]

Therefore, rewards and advantages should be detached from the policy loss.

What is missing from the basic objective?

Maximizing a learned reward alone commonly causes:

  • entropy collapse;

  • large movement away from the SFT model;

  • reward-model exploitation;

  • repetitive high-scoring patterns;

  • degraded capabilities not measured by reward.

Practical RLHF therefore adds KL control, critic/value training, PPO-style update limits and independent evaluations. We will add KL control next.

Tensor shapes in today’s response-level abstraction:

  • response logits: [responses]

  • sampled response IDs: [B]

  • log-probabilities, rewards and advantages: [B]

  • loss: scalar

DPO distinction

DPO trains on fixed chosen/rejected pairs. Reward-model RLHF generates fresh responses, scores them and improves the current policy using RL.

Both originate from preference data, but only the second contains an online rollout-and-reward loop.

🔍 Deeper: the RLHF objective

\[\max_\theta\ \mathbb E_{x\sim\mathcal D,\,y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)}\big[r_\phi(x,y)\big]-\beta\,\mathbb E_x\big[\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot|x)\big)\big].\] In practice the KL term is folded into a per-token reward: \(r_t=-\beta\big(\log\pi_\theta(y_t|\cdot)-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t|\cdot)\big)+\mathbb 1[t=T]\,r_\phi(x,y)\).

It’s on-policy: the expectation is over \(y\sim\pi_\theta\). After each update, the old samples describe a slightly different policy. That’s why the loop keeps regenerating, and why PPO’s ratios appear.

🔑 Key ideas

  • RLHF = sample \(\to\) score \(\to\) update, repeated, with a KL leash.

  • The reward model is frozen, but the data distribution keeps moving.

🚶 Walkthrough: shaped per-token rewards

Response of 3 tokens, \(\log\pi_\theta-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}=(0.2,-0.1,0.3)\), \(\beta=0.1\), RM score \(1.0\). \(r=(-0.02,\ +0.01,\ -0.03+1.0)=(-0.02,0.01,0.97)\). Sequence KL penalty \(=0.1\cdot0.4=0.04\).

Common mistakes

  • Applying KL to prompt tokens.

  • Computing \(\log\pi_{\text{ref}}-\log\pi_\theta\) (the wrong sign).

  • Adding the RM score to every token instead of once at the end.

⚙️ Algorithm: RLHF with PPO
Input: SFT model \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (frozen), reward model \(r_\phi\) (frozen), KL coefficient \(\beta\)
\(\pi_\theta\gets\pi_{\text{ref}}\); initialize value head \(V_\psi\)
repeat
sample prompts \(x\) and responses \(y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)\)
score each response: \(s\gets r_\phi(x,y)\)
per-token reward \(r_t\gets-\beta\big(\log\pi_\theta(y_t|\cdot)-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t|\cdot)\big)+\mathbb 1[t=T]\,s\)
compute GAE advantages over response tokens; run the LLM PPO update (Topic 30)
until the reward model and independent evals stop improving

0: weak response
1: acceptable response
2: reward-model favourite

This isolates the loop without loading an LLM.

Hints

  • Sample using policy.sample((batch_size,)).

  • Baseline: subtract the batch’s mean reward.

  • Normalize with population standard deviation and 1e-8.

  • Use policy.log_prob(sampled_responses).

  • Optimizers minimize, so use a leading negative sign.

  • favourite_rate tests whether the sampled ID equals 2.

Debugging experiments

First, remove the negative sign:

policy_loss = (
    log_probs * advantages
).mean()

Gradient descent now reduces the probability of above-average responses.

Then move response sampling outside the update loop. Every update reuses actions generated by the initial policy without importance ratios or PPO controls. The data becomes increasingly off-policy as policy_logits changes.

Solutions

Generate fresh policy responses:

sampled_responses = policy.sample(
    (batch_size,)
)

Calculate normalized advantages:

advantages = rewards - rewards.mean()

advantages = advantages / (
    advantages.std(unbiased=False) + 1e-8
)

Selected log-probabilities:

log_probs = policy.log_prob(
    sampled_responses
)

Policy loss:

policy_loss = -(
    log_probs * advantages.detach()
).mean()

Favourite-response rate:

favourite_rate = (
    sampled_responses == 2
).float().mean()

Initially:

\[\pi=[1/3,1/3,1/3]\]

The average reward is initially near:

\[\frac{0+1+2}{3}=1\]

Response 2 usually receives a positive advantage, response 0 a negative advantage, and response 1 an advantage near zero. Training therefore shifts probability toward response 2.

The policy should approach:

P(response 0) → near 0
P(response 1) → near 0
P(response 2) → near 1

Entropy begins at:

\[\mathcal H(\pi)=\log3\approx1.099\]

and falls as the policy collapses toward the reward-model favourite.

The mean reward need not rise monotonically because both responses and reward noise are sampled. Track moving averages rather than interpreting individual batches.

Check answers

  1. Humans provide the preference comparisons used to train the reward model; they may also provide later evaluations or updated preference data.

  2. Sampling is discrete, and the policy-gradient estimator uses reward as a fixed weight on the sampled response’s log-probability.

  3. The policy may exploit reward-model errors or sacrifice qualities absent from its training labels.

  4. A baseline reduces policy-gradient variance; a critic learns a prompt- or prefix-dependent baseline and supports token-level advantage estimation.

Retrieval answers

  1. \(P(y_w\succ y_l)=\sigma(r_w-r_l)\).

  2. Padding is not a generated action, so it must contribute neither rewards nor gradients.

Takeaway: reward-model RLHF is a changing-data loop: generate with the current policy, score those generations, then update their likelihoods. A frozen reward model can guide learning, but optimizing it without policy constraints invites collapse and exploitation.

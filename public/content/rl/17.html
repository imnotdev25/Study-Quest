🧩 Analogy: election polling

A poll accidentally surveys twice as many young people as there are in the population. Pollsters fix this by reweighting: each young respondent counts \(\times0.5\). Importance sampling does exactly that: data came from policy \(b\), we care about policy \(\pi\), so weight each sample by \(\pi/b\).

Reweighting only works if the poll reached some of every group. If nobody over 70 answered, no weighting can fix it.

Where \(\pi\) is high but \(b\) is low, the rare samples get huge weights \(\pi/b\).

2. Correcting a mismatched sampling distribution

Suppose a survey oversamples one city. Its raw average will not represent the whole country. You can correct it by giving each response a weight based on:

\[\frac{\text{desired population probability}} {\text{sampled population probability}}\]

In RL:

  • \(\mu(a\mid s)\): behaviour policy that generated the data.

  • \(\pi(a\mid s)\): target policy we want to evaluate or improve.

The importance ratio is:

\[\rho_t = \frac{\pi(A_t\mid S_t)} {\mu(A_t\mid S_t)}\]

It converts an expectation under \(\mu\) into one under \(\pi\):

\[\mathbb E_{A\sim\pi}[f(A)] = \mathbb E_{A\sim\mu} \left[ \rho(A)f(A) \right]\]

Interpret the weight:

  • \(\rho>1\): the sampled action is more common under the target policy, so amplify it.

  • \(\rho<1\): it is less common under the target policy, so reduce it.

  • \(\rho=1\): both policies assign it equal probability.

The support requirement

Importance sampling only works if:

\[\pi(a\mid s)>0 \implies \mu(a\mid s)>0\]

If the target policy might choose an action that the behaviour policy never sampled, stored data contains no evidence about that action. No finite weight can recover missing experience.

Why variance can explode

For a complete trajectory, the correction may contain a product:

\[\rho_{0:T} = \prod_{t=0}^{T} \frac{\pi(A_t\mid S_t)} {\mu(A_t\mid S_t)}\]

Even moderate per-step ratios can create enormous or tiny trajectory weights. A few rare samples may dominate the estimate.

Compute ratios in log-space:

\[\log\rho_t = \log\pi(A_t\mid S_t) - \log\mu(A_t\mid S_t)\]

\[\rho_t=\exp(\log\rho_t)\]

Useful diagnostics include maximum ratio and effective sample size:

\[\operatorname{ESS} = \frac{(\sum_i\rho_i)^2} {\sum_i\rho_i^2}\]

ESS near batch size means balanced weights. Low ESS means only a few samples meaningfully contribute.

For a batch:

  • actions: [B]

  • behaviour and target log-probabilities: [B]

  • importance ratios and rewards: [B]

  • weighted estimate: scalar

LLM connection

An LLM rollout may be generated by an “old” policy and reused after parameters change. The stored old log-probabilities let us calculate:

\[\rho_t = \exp\left( \log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid y_{<t}) - \log\pi_{\text{old}}(y_t\mid y_{<t}) \right)\]

PPO uses these ratios but restricts how strongly they can change the policy. That restriction comes later.

🔍 Deeper: unbiased, but the variance can explode

\(\mathbb E_{x\sim\pi}[f(x)]=\sum_x b(x)\frac{\pi(x)}{b(x)}f(x)=\mathbb E_{x\sim b}[\rho(x)f(x)]\), with \(\rho=\pi/b\). This needs \(b(x)>0\) wherever \(\pi(x)>0\).

Variance: \(\mathbb E_b[\rho^2]=1+\chi^2(\pi\|b)\). Over a trajectory, the ratios multiply, so the second moment grows like \((1+c)^T\):

Diagnostics: effective sample size \(\text{ESS}=(\sum w_i)^2/\sum w_i^2\). Weighted (self-normalized) IS divides by \(\sum w_i\) instead of \(N\): slightly biased, much lower variance.

🔑 Key ideas

  • Reweight by \(\pi/b\) to reuse someone else’s data.

  • Products of ratios explode, which is why PPO keeps \(\pi\) close to \(b\).

🚶 Walkthrough: IS can produce impossible values

\(b=(0.5,0.5)\), \(\pi=(0.9,0.1)\), rewards \(r(a_1)=1\), \(r(a_2)=0\). True value: \(0.9\). Logged actions: \(a_1,a_1,a_1,a_2\). Weights: \(1.8,1.8,1.8,0.2\). Ordinary IS \(=\frac{1.8\cdot3}{4}=1.35\), which is above the maximum possible reward of 1. Weighted IS \(=\frac{5.4}{5.6}=0.964\). ESS \(=5.6^2/9.76\approx3.2\).

Common mistakes

  • Flipping the ratio to \(b/\pi\).

  • Using \(\pi\)’s probabilities from after it was updated, for data it never sampled.

  • Ignoring actions where \(b=0\) but \(\pi>0\) (no correction can fix those).

⚙️ Algorithm: Importance-sampling estimate of a target policy
Input: samples \((x_i,a_i,r_i)_{i=1}^N\) collected by \(b\), probabilities \(b(a_i|x_i)>0\), target \(\pi\)
for \(i=1,\dots,N\) do
\(w_i\gets\pi(a_i|x_i)/b(a_i|x_i)\)
end
\(\hat V_{\text{IS}}\gets\frac1N\sum_iw_ir_i\) ▷ unbiased
\(\hat V_{\text{WIS}}\gets\sum_iw_ir_i\big/\sum_iw_i\) ▷ self-normalized: small bias, lower variance
\(\text{ESS}\gets(\sum_iw_i)^2/\sum_iw_i^2\)
return \(\hat V_{\text{IS}},\hat V_{\text{WIS}},\text{ESS}\)

Hints

  • Use each distribution’s log_prob(actions).

  • Self-normalized IS divides the weighted reward sum by the weight sum.

  • ESS uses squared weights, not squared rewards.

Debugging experiment

Reverse the log-ratio:

log_ratios = log_mu - log_pi

This weights samples in precisely the wrong direction: actions already common under the behaviour policy receive even more influence.

Also try only 200 samples. The rare but heavily weighted target-favoured action may appear too few times, making the estimate unstable despite mathematically correct weights.

4. Checks for understanding

  1. Why is importance sampling needed for off-policy policy evaluation?

  2. Why can’t it recover an action whose behaviour probability is zero?

  3. Why are ratios calculated from log-probabilities?

  4. Does an unbiased estimator necessarily have low variance?

Solutions

Generate behaviour data:

actions = behavior.sample((batch_size,))

Selected log-probabilities:

log_mu = behavior.log_prob(actions)
log_pi = target.log_prob(actions)

Importance ratios:

log_ratios = log_pi - log_mu
ratios = log_ratios.exp()

Ordinary estimate:

is_estimate = (
    ratios * rewards
).mean()

Self-normalized estimate:

self_normalized_estimate = (
    ratios * rewards
).sum() / ratios.sum().clamp_min(1e-8)

Effective sample size:

effective_sample_size = (
    ratios.sum().pow(2)
    / ratios.pow(2).sum().clamp_min(1e-8)
)

Approximate policy probabilities are reversed:

Behaviour: [0.844, 0.114, 0.042]
Target:    [0.042, 0.114, 0.844]

Therefore, action 2 is rare in the collected data but receives the largest weight:

\[\rho(a=2) \approx \frac{0.844}{0.042} \approx20\]

The exact target value is approximately:

\[0.042(-1)+0.114(0)+0.844(2) \approx1.65\]

The naive estimate should instead be near the behaviour policy’s negative expected reward. Both importance-sampling estimates should approach 1.65, but they may fluctuate because relatively few samples of action 2 carry large weights.

Under the support condition:

\[\mathbb E_{A\sim\mu}[\rho(A)]=1\]

so the sampled mean ratio should be near one. ESS will be substantially below 20,000 because the weights are highly uneven.

Ordinary IS is unbiased under standard assumptions. Self-normalized IS is generally biased at finite sample sizes, but often has lower variance.

Check answers

  1. Raw behaviour data reflects \(\mu\), while the desired expectation is under \(\pi\); ratios correct that distribution mismatch.

  2. No collected sample represents that action, and division by zero would require an infinite weight.

  3. Subtracting log-probabilities is more numerically stable than directly dividing tiny probabilities.

  4. No. Importance sampling can be unbiased while having extremely large or even unbounded variance.

Retrieval answers

  1. The behaviour policy generates experience; the target policy is the policy being evaluated or improved.

  2. GAE becomes the one-step TD advantage: \(\hat A_t=\delta_t\).

Takeaway: importance sampling says, “This experience came from one policy, but how representative is it of another?” The correction enables off-policy learning, while weight variance determines whether that correction is practically useful.

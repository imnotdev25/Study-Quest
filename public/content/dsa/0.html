👋 Intro

The roles (ML Engineer, AI Engineer, Applied Scientist, Research Engineer, Research Scientist), the rounds in a typical loop, what each round actually scores, how big tech and AI labs differ, and how levels change expectations.

A note on sources.

Interview loops change often and vary by team. This chapter combines what companies publish about their own processes (for example Amazon’s applied scientist interview prep page and Anthropic’s candidate guidance on AI use) with widely shared community resources (Chip Huyen’s Introduction to Machine Learning Interviews, the Machine-Learning-Interviews guide by A. Dadashzadeh based on offers from Meta, Google, Amazon, and Apple). Treat specifics as typical patterns, and always ask your recruiter for the exact loop.

The roles this book targets

🌱 Beginner

Think of the loop as a set of different exams, each graded by a different teacher. One checks whether you can solve a new problem with code (DSA). One checks whether you know ML deeply (breadth/depth). One checks whether you can design a real system (ML system design). One checks whether you would be a good colleague (behavioral). You need to pass most of them, not ace one.

Anatomy of a Typical Loop

The common shape of ML and AI interview loops.

🔧 Intermediate

The six round types and what they score:

  1. DSA coding. Classic algorithm problems (Parts II–III). Scored on: clarifying the problem, choosing data structures, a correct solution, complexity analysis, clean code, testing, and communication. Community guides recommend 300–400 deeply reviewed problems organized by pattern, not by memorizing answers.

  2. ML coding. Implement an ML algorithm or component from scratch, usually with NumPy or plain Python: k-means, kNN, logistic regression with gradient descent, stable softmax and cross-entropy, ROC-AUC, attention, BPE, top-p sampling, a KV cache, or a training loop (Chapters 17–18). Scored on shapes, numerical stability, vectorization, correctness, and tests.

  3. ML breadth / depth. Rapid-fire fundamentals (bias-variance, regularization, metrics, optimizers, transformers) followed by a deep dive into one area or your past project. “Why” questions dominate.

  4. ML / GenAI system design. Design a recommendation, search, ranking, fraud, or RAG/agent system end to end (Chapter 20). For GenAI designs, evaluation methodology, cost, latency, and guardrails matter more than boxes.

  5. Behavioral. Past experiences in STAR format: conflict, failure, impact, ambiguity, leadership. Amazon structures this around its Leadership Principles and asks two or three such questions in every round.

  6. Role-specific. Research talk (researchers), low-level/object-oriented design (some MLE and AI engineer loops, Chapter 19), practical debugging, or a take-home project.

Company Patterns

Company type Commonly reported patterns
Google Generalist hiring: several DSA rounds, ML domain round(s), behavioral (“Googleyness and leadership”). Hiring committee reviews written feedback; team matching often happens after the committee.
Meta MLE loop typically has two coding rounds, ML system design (sometimes two at senior levels), and behavioral. Coding rounds are fast-paced: often two problems in 45 minutes, so speed of pattern recognition matters.
Amazon Per Amazon’s prep page for applied scientists: one or two 60-minute phone screens, then a loop of four 55-minute interviews covering technical topics, problem solving and coding, knowledge depth and breadth, a tech talk, and Leadership Principles in every round. One interviewer is a “Bar Raiser” from outside the team.
Apple, Microsoft, Netflix More team-specific: interviews often reflect the team’s domain (speech, vision, recommendations), with DSA, ML depth, and a project deep dive. Netflix emphasizes senior judgment and culture.
AI labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, others) Practical coding more than puzzles: build or debug something realistic, often multi-part and progressively harder; ML implementation; systems and scaling; research or project discussion; strong focus on mission and values. Anthropic’s published guidance: AI assistance is not allowed in live interviews unless stated, encouraged for preparation.

How loops differ (verify with your recruiter; they change)

🧠 Expert

The “practical coding” style at AI labs and newer companies looks different from LeetCode. Typical shapes: implement a small in-memory key-value store with transactions, then add TTLs, then make it thread-safe; parse and aggregate logs; implement a tokenizer or a simple autograd engine; debug a training loop whose loss is NaN; build a rate limiter or a job scheduler. The algorithms are the same ones in this book, but the scoring rewards clean abstractions, incremental extension, and testing under changing requirements. Chapter 19 (low-level design) prepares you directly for this.

🔧 Intermediate

Trends across reports:

  • AI-assisted coding rounds are appearing at some big-tech loops: you may be given an AI assistant and judged on problem decomposition, verifying its output, and testing, not on typing speed. Practice reviewing generated code critically.

  • AI labs favor practical, extensible coding (key-value stores, schedulers, parsers, LLM utilities) over puzzles; clean structure under changing requirements and concurrency matter (Chapter 19).

  • Clarifying questions are part of the score: the Amazon cache discussion above began with what exactly to store.

  • ML loops mix classic DSA with ML design; recommendation and ranking prompts dominate ML system design reports.

  • Behavioral is consistent: proudest project, failure, conflict, growth.

How Interviewers Score

🔧 Intermediate

Most companies use a rubric with four or five dimensions per round, each rated on a scale, plus an overall hire/no-hire signal. For coding:

  • Problem solving: did you explore approaches, recognize the pattern, and reach an optimal solution with limited hints?

  • Coding: is the code correct, readable, and well structured?

  • Verification: did you test with examples, edge cases, and find your own bugs?

  • Communication: could the interviewer follow your reasoning the whole time?

A correct solution with silent, unexplained thinking often scores lower than a slightly slower solution that was explained clearly. Interviewers write down evidence; give them evidence to write.

⚠️ Pitfall

  • Memorizing solutions: a single twist in the problem exposes it immediately.

  • Jumping into code before confirming the problem and the approach.

  • Neglecting ML coding: many MLE candidates prepare only LeetCode and fail “implement k-means.”

  • Treating behavioral as an afterthought; it can veto an otherwise strong loop.

  • Using AI tools during a live interview where it is not permitted.

🔑 Takeaways

  • Loops share the same building blocks: DSA, ML coding, ML breadth/depth, ML system design, behavioral, plus role-specific rounds.

  • Prepare by pattern and by understanding, not by memorized solutions.

  • AI labs favor practical, multi-part coding; big tech favors classic DSA plus ML design.

  • Seniority shifts weight from “can solve” to “chooses well and explains trade-offs.”
